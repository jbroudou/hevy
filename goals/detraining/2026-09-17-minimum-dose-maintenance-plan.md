# Minimum-Dose Maintenance Plan

**Prepared 17 Sep 2026.** The question this plan answers: *when training has to
stop or shrink, how do we slow the rate at which fitness is lost?*

It is a standing plan, not a camp plan. It applies during travel, illness,
work pressure, injury and the post-fight break, and it is explicitly **not** an
alternative to the current camp prescriptions in
[../vo2-recovery/README.md](../vo2-recovery/README.md) and
[../shoulder-rehab/README.md](../shoulder-rehab/README.md). Running it while
normal training is possible would under-train.

Every paper below was read this session, not recalled. Where only a summary
could be retrieved, that is stated.

## The one-line answer

**Keep the intensity; cut the frequency and the volume.** Across the whole
maintenance literature, intensity is the variable that must be preserved.
Frequency and volume can be cut by a third to two-thirds for months with no
measurable loss — but cutting intensity loses the adaptation even when
frequency and volume are untouched
([Spiering et al. 2021](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2021/05000/maintaining_physical_performance__the_minimal_dose.35.aspx),
*J Strength Cond Res* 35(5):1449–1458).

The most direct demonstration is Hickson's series, which Spiering reviews.
Subjects trained 6 sessions/week, 40 min, at ~90–100% HRmax for 10 weeks, then
had **one** variable cut for the next 15 weeks:

| What was cut | By how much | Result at 15 weeks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frequency | 6 → 4 or 2 sessions/week | VO2max and short-term endurance **fully maintained**, either way |
| Volume | 40 → 26 min (−33%) | VO2max, short- and long-term endurance **fully maintained** |
| Volume | 40 → 13 min (−66%) | VO2max and short-term endurance maintained; long-term endurance **only partly** |
| Intensity | 90–100% → 82–87% HRmax (−33%) | VO2max **not maintained** |
| Intensity | 90–100% → 61–67% HRmax (−66%) | Most of the gain **lost**, despite still training 6 × 40 min/week |

The last row is the one to remember: six sessions a week of 40 minutes, done
easy, did not hold what two hard sessions of 13 minutes did.

## What decays, and how fast

Two different clocks. Aerobic fitness starts falling within about a week;
strength holds for weeks and then falls slowly.

### Aerobic

Collated in [Frontiers in Physiology 2024, *Cardiorespiratory and metabolic
consequences of detraining in endurance
athletes*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1334766/full)
from the primary studies named:

| Days stopped | VO2max | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 12 | −7% | Coyle 1984 |
| 14 | −4.7% | Houmard 1992 |
| 15 | −3.7% | Chen 2022 |
| 21 | −4.2% | Martin 1986 |
| 35 | −10.1% | García-Pallarés 2009 |
| 56 | −13% | Coyle 1984 |
| ~60 | −20% | Martin 1986 |

What is behind those numbers matters more than the numbers:

- **Blood and plasma volume go first and come back fastest.** Plasma volume
  −5.1% at 14 days; blood volume −9% and plasma −12% at 2–4 weeks. This is
  most of the first fortnight's VO2max loss, and it is restored within days of
  training again.
- **Mitochondrial enzyme activity is the expensive loss.** Citrate synthase
  −5% at 6 days, −29% at 10 days, −40% at 56 days. This is not restored in
  days.
- Stroke volume −14% at 56 days; maximal heart rate rises 2–9 bpm.

For a 46-year-old this compounds with age. In masters endurance athletes
([*The Impact of Training on the Loss of Cardiorespiratory Fitness in Aging
Masters Endurance
Athletes*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9517884/)), VO2max falls
**−5 to −6.5% per decade** in those who hold their training volume, **−8 to
−26% per decade** with an 11–20% cut, and **−15 to −46% per decade** with a cut
over 20% or a return to sedentary. Change in training volume explains 54% of
the variance in men's decline. The same review puts 12 weeks of cessation at
about −18% — **roughly a decade of ageing, bought in three months.**

### Strength and power

[McMaster et al. 2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-013-0031-3),
*Sports Med* 43:367–384, systematic review in elite rugby union, rugby league
and American football:

- Maximal strength and power are **maintained for about 3 weeks** with no
  training at all.
- After that: strength ~**2% per week**, power **0.1–0.6% per week**.
- Pooled (n = 155): **−14.5 ± 14.3%** strength after a mean 7.2 ± 5.8 weeks of
  cessation. Note that the spread is as large as the mean — individual
  responses vary enormously.

[Bickel et al. 2011](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Exercise%20Dosing%20to%20Retain%20Resistance%20Training%20Adaptations%20in%20Young%20and%20Older%20Adults.pdf),
*Med Sci Sports Exerc* 43(7):1177–1187, is the cleanest dosing study. Seventy
adults, young (20–35) and older (60–75), trained 3 d/week for 16 weeks, then
spent **32 weeks** either detrained, on a **one-third dose** (same 3 sets,
frequency 3 → 1 d/week) or a **one-ninth dose** (1 d/week and 3 sets → 1 set):

- Young: **both** doses preserved the hypertrophy; the one-third dose added
  more.
- Older: **neither** dose preserved myofiber hypertrophy.
- **Strength was largely retained even through 32 weeks of complete
  detraining**, with only a slight fall at the final time point.

Spiering's synthesis of the strength literature: dropping from 2–3 sessions a
week to **1 session a week maintains or even increases 1RM for up to 32 weeks**,
as long as the load is unchanged; 1 set per exercise on 1 session a week holds
strength in young and old, and size in the young; older populations need about
2 sessions a week and 2–3 sets to hold size. Rønnestad's professional
footballers held strength and sprint speed on **1 session a week for 12 weeks**,
and less than 1 session a week was not enough.

### Where the evidence runs out

- Spiering's underlying studies used **relatively untrained general-population
  participants**. He says so explicitly, and concludes there are insufficient
  data to make specific recommendations for athletes. For highly trained
  people, other authors he cites recommend cutting frequency by **no more than
  ~20%**.
- Bickel's age brackets are 20–35 and 60–75. **A 46-year-old is in neither.**
  Treating him as "older" for muscle size is a judgement, not a finding.
- Nothing here covers **skill**. Punch timing, distance and defensive reflex
  decay is not in this literature, and boxing is not recorded in either system,
  so it cannot be tracked here either.
- Spiering also excludes power, agility and flexibility from his review for
  lack of data.

## Three protocols

### A. Reduced time — the maintenance week

Use when training is possible but time is not. About **2.5 hours a week**.

| Slot | Session | The rule that matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cardio 1 | Hard intervals — the 4×4 bike session already prescribed | Intensity unchanged: work 161–170 bpm. Cut the warm-up and the filler, never the reps |
| Cardio 2 | Second hard session, or a sparring round-set if boxing is on | Same. Two hard sessions a week is the endurance floor |
| Strength | One full-body session, 5–6 exercises, 2–3 sets each | **Loads unchanged.** 3–4 reps in reserve. One session is the floor; two is better at 46 |
| Daily | ≥7,000 steps on non-training days | See below |

Substituting easy sessions for hard ones because the week is busy is the exact
condition that failed in Hickson's intensity arm. If only one session fits,
make it a hard one.

**Steps are not filler** — see *Between sessions* below for why, and for the
desk question.

### B. Complete stop

Use when training is genuinely not possible — illness, travel, acute injury,
the post-fight break.

| Length | What it costs | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ≤7 days | Essentially nothing that isn't plasma volume | Nothing. Take it |
| 8–14 days | ~4–7% VO2max, most of it plasma volume, back within days of restarting | Acceptable. Walk daily |
| 15–28 days | Mitochondrial territory: citrate synthase is already −29% by day 10 | Get one hard session in. One session breaks the clock |
| >28 days | 10%+ VO2max, and rising toward −18% at 12 weeks | Re-entry needs a ramp, not a resumption |

**Post-fight:** a deliberate 7–14 day full stop is cheap and worth taking. Then
straight into the maintenance week — not into nothing.

**Re-entry after a stop:** restore **intensity early and volume last**. The
aerobic system recovers faster than the tissue that carries it: the repo's
standing injury risk is calf and Achilles tolerance, from 34.1 km in 17 days on
a base of seven outdoor runs in the preceding 33 weeks. Cap the first week back
at roughly half the pre-layoff volume, with one session at full intensity.

### C. Short breaks — four days to a week

**Nothing is at risk, and the correct dose is none.** No study in this set even
measures a break that short. The earliest measurement points anywhere in the
evidence above are citrate synthase at 6 days, VO2max at 12 days, plasma volume
at 14 days, and strength at about 3 weeks. Four days sits below every one of
them.

A short break is also often the useful thing rather than the cost. A week that
contains an over-intense run, an irritated shoulder and a drifting resting HR
is a week where four days off buys more than four days of micro-efforts would.

So the risk in a short break is not physiological, it is behavioural: **four
days becoming four weeks.** The record says so plainly — median gap between
recorded cardio sessions 14 days, longest 39. Two rules follow:

1. **Book the session that ends the break before the break starts.**
2. **Spend the window on what keeps getting skipped.** Shoulder rehab (3/week
   prescribed, 3 sessions in 22 days) and the calf/soleus routine (2–3/week
   prescribed, performed once) are both low-fatigue and cost the break nothing.

If an intensity anchor is wanted, stair "snacks" — 2–3 × 20–30 s hard climbs,
an hour or more apart — are the cheapest one
([Jenkins et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-019-04110-z),
~5–12% VO2peak gains). So are daily maximal contractions: one 3-second maximal
eccentric contraction a day raised elbow-flexor strength >10% in four weeks
([Nosaka's group](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/942387), *SJMSS*).
**Both were done in sedentary or untrained people, building fitness from a low
base over weeks — neither shows anything about a trained athlete maintaining
over days.** Treat them as habit anchors, not as a stimulus. [I]

## Between sessions: steps, the desk and NEAT

Everything above is about sessions. This section is about the other 23 hours,
which matter for a reason independent of training: **step reduction is itself a
loss stimulus.** Fourteen days of it lowered postprandial muscle protein
synthesis ~26% and cost leg lean mass in healthy elderly
([Breen et al. 2013](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/98/6/2604/2537326),
*JCEM* 98(6):2604–2612). A 2024 study in older adults found more limited
effects, so the magnitude is contested, and both cohorts are far older than this
athlete. The direction is consistent and acting on it costs nothing. [L, I]

This is a live deficit, not a theoretical one: 1,497, 1,422 and 2,326 steps on
6, 8 and 9 Sep — near-sedentary days between hard sessions. [C]

### Standing desk vs walking pad

| | vs sitting | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Standing** | **+0.15 kcal/min** (~9 kcal/h); 6 h/day ≈ 54 kcal/day at 65 kg. **No extra steps** | [46 studies, 1,184 participants](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29385357/) |
| **Walking pad / treadmill desk** | **~+105 kcal/h** in lab conditions; **~+2,000 steps/day** actually observed in workplaces | [13 studies, 351 participants](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8590128/) |

Standing is a posture change, not an activity — nine calories an hour and no
steps. Its real evidence base is lower back and neck symptoms and reduced
sedentary time, which are good reasons to own one but not detraining reasons.
The walking pad is the one that moves the number this goal cares about: 2 h at
3 km/h is mechanically ~9,000 steps, and even the far lower figure people
actually achieve (~+2,000/day) turns a near-bed-rest day into an ordinary one.

**What it does not do:**

- **It will not maintain VO2max.** Desk pace runs ~90–105 bpm against a Zone 2
  band of 126–141; the 2 Sep treadmill walk needed gradient 6 at 6 km/h to reach
  138. Low-intensity volume is exactly the condition that failed in Hickson's
  intensity arm. It is NEAT, not a session, and must not be logged as the easy
  Zone 2 session. [C, L]
- **It will not maintain strength.** Nothing about walking approaches the load
  that holds a 120 kg deadlift. [I]

**Load caveat.** Going from ~2,000 to ~9,000 steps a day overnight is a real
cumulative-load increase on the calf and Achilles — the repo's standing injury
risk, at 91 kg, with a documented calf/soleus deficit that already makes incline
walking fail before HR rises. The intensity is trivial; the volume is not. Ramp
it: 30–45 min/day for a week or two, then build, and keep the calf/soleus
routine rather than treating the pad as a substitute for it. No study covers
walking-desk load in a heavier athlete with a calf deficit — this is inference.
[I]

**Verdict:** a walking pad under a height-adjustable desk gives sitting,
standing and walking. For this goal it is the pad that earns its place; a
standing desk alone is bought for the back, not for VO2max. [I]

## What we will measure

COROS VO2max (41) is too coarse and too slow to see any of this, and the only
thing that updates it — the outdoor Running Fitness Test — would overwrite the
correct threshold HR and max HR settings. So it is not the benchmark.

Three cheap, repeatable readings instead, all with a baseline already in the
repo or obtainable in one session:

| Benchmark | Protocol | Baseline | Reads |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aerobic | Treadmill, gradient 6 at 6 km/h, 15 min unbroken; record HR at minute 15 | 2 Sep: HR rose 122 → 138 across the walk | Aerobic decay, without costing recovery or trusting indoor pace |
| Strength | Deadlift, 4 reps at 120 kg, reps-in-reserve logged | 14 Sep (W4): 4 × 4 @ 120 kg | Strength decay |
| Systemic | Resting HR on waking, on-watch Wellness Check | 56 bpm (16 Sep) | Whole-body state |

Take all three **before** the next layoff starts, and again on the first day
back. Without a before-reading, a layoff teaches nothing.

## The rules, in short

1. Intensity is not negotiable. Frequency and volume are.
2. Two hard cardio sessions a week hold the aerobic system. One a fortnight
   does not.
3. One heavy strength session a week holds strength for months. Loads
   unchanged.
4. No cardio gap longer than 14 days, ever. Target 7.
5. Walk on the days you don't train.
6. Coming back: intensity first, volume last.
7. A break under a week needs no defence. Book the session that ends it before
   it starts.
