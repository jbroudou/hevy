Energy systems we discussed
Aerobic system

This is the foundation of boxing conditioning.

Although individual exchanges are explosive, boxing is predominantly aerobic when total energy expenditure across rounds is considered.

The aerobic system helps:

sustain movement throughout the fight
restore phosphocreatine between explosive combinations
recover during quieter periods within rounds
recover during the 1-minute break
delay fatigue

Key concept:

The anaerobic systems produce the explosions; the aerobic system helps recharge them.

Anaerobic-alactic system

Very high-power energy available immediately.

Typical boxing example:

2–5 second explosive combination

Characteristics:

extremely powerful
very short duration
mainly ATP/phosphocreatine
little lactate production
Anaerobic glycolytic system

Becomes increasingly important if the intense exchange continues.

Typical example:

10–30 seconds of sustained hard punching, defending and movement

Characteristics:

lasts longer than the alactic system
produces substantially more metabolic fatigue
associated with burning/heavy arms and declining power

The systems do not switch on and off abruptly. They overlap.

A simplified sequence is:

short explosive burst → predominantly alactic

If the effort continues:

glycolytic contribution rises

Throughout this:

aerobic metabolism is also contributing and helping recovery.

Hard sparring should count as a hard conditioning session, rather than simply adding more hard intervals on top of it.

Zone 2

The objective is to build aerobic capacity without generating large amounts of fatigue.

Recommended options:

stationary bike
incline treadmill walking
easy running if well tolerated

The conclusion was that trying to make Zone 2 artificially harder with:

weighted vests
hand weights
breathing restriction
forced nasal-only breathing

isn't supported as being superior.

The most evidence-supported ways of improving Zone 2 adaptations are:

correct intensity + adequate duration + sufficient weekly volume + consistency

Rather than making 40 minutes harder:

40 → 45 → 50 → 60 minutes

can be a better progression.

Why incline walking instead of always running?

Running is absolutely capable of producing Zone 2 adaptations.

Incline walking was suggested because the same cardiovascular workload can often be created with:

less repetitive impact and lower musculoskeletal recovery cost.

This matters because the athlete also needs resources for:

boxing
sparring
strength work
explosive training
hard cardiovascular intervals

The principle is:

Make the heart work without unnecessarily exhausting the legs.

Running can still be used if:

joints tolerate it
legs recover well
it does not interfere with boxing or strength work

Improving recovery between explosive combinations

For only 5–10 seconds of recovery between attacks, full phosphocreatine restoration is impossible.

The objective is to improve rapid partial recovery.

Priority factors discussed:

Aerobic fitness
Repeated short-burst training
Learning to relax immediately after combinations
Creatine / adequate nutrition and fueling

Boxing-specific example:

3 sec maximum punching
10 sec active/easy recovery

repeat approximately 10–14 times

The recovery period should actually be recovery:

move
breathe
loosen shoulders
stay relaxed
jab/feint lightly if appropriate

rather than continuing to work maximally.

Repeated explosive work

A particularly relevant boxer-specific protocol discussed was approximately:

3 seconds maximal punching
10 seconds recovery

repeated through a round.

Example:

14 repetitions
for
3 rounds
with
1 minute between rounds

Purpose:

train:

explode → recover → explode

rather than:

punch continuously until the arms fail.

This targets repeated high-power output more closely than continuous maximal bag punching.

Respiratory muscle training

We clarified the terminology:

resisted breathing, not restricted breathing.

The athlete should not deliberately restrict oxygen.

The idea is to strengthen the inspiratory muscles, particularly the diaphragm, by breathing against mechanical resistance.

Suggested device type:

POWERbreathe or similar inspiratory muscle trainer

Not:

altitude mask
oxygen-restriction mask
incentive spirometer
mucus-clearance device

Typical protocol discussed:

30 resisted inspirations
twice daily
5–6 days/week

with progressive resistance over several weeks.

Expected effect is mainly:

stronger/more fatigue-resistant breathing muscles

rather than a major increase in VO₂max.

Possible boxing benefits:

breathing feels less overwhelming late in rounds
faster return to controlled breathing
easier deep breaths between exchanges
respiratory muscles become less of the limiting factor

Evidence is promising for increased inspiratory muscle strength, but evidence for meaningful sports-performance improvements is less certain, so this is considered a small accessory, not a major conditioning pillar.

Overall training philosophy

The desired five-round pattern is:

Round 1: powerful and relaxed
Round 2: powerful and relaxed
Round 3: working hard but technically sound
Round 4: significant cardiovascular pressure without breakdown
Round 5: tired but still capable of producing fast, powerful attacks

The main mistake to avoid is trying to make every training session maximally fight-specific or maximally exhausting.

Different sessions have different jobs:

Zone 2 → aerobic recovery machinery

Hard intervals → aerobic ceiling

Repeated short bursts → explosive repeatability

Strength/power → force and explosiveness

Sparring → integration into actual fighting

The program should therefore optimise the total weekly training load, not simply accumulate as many hard sessions as possible.
